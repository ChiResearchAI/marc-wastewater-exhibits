Forecast Validation Bench

What a wastewater forecast can and cannot be asked to do, measured by rolling-origin backtest on 79 Kansas and Missouri sewersheds — 12,593 site-weeks of current CDC wastewater data, through 5 October 2026. Including the sewersheds where the model loses to a flat line.

Data source
CDC NWSS public wastewater metrics, data.cdc.gov dataset j9g8-acpt (the release that superseded the archived g653-rqe2 on 12 Sep 2025)
Scope
All Kansas and Missouri records in that release: 24,408 records across 102 sites; 80 met the inclusion rule, of which 79 produced enough scored forecasts to report (8 KS, 71 MO)
Series span
July 2020 – October 2026
Reporting sources
NWSS, WastewaterSCAN, CDC/Verily — mixed, as published
Design
Expanding-origin backtest, weekly grid, horizons of 1, 2 and 4 weeks
Reference
Persistence — last observed weekly value

What this is. A method demonstration built from public data, prepared under Section 5.1 of MARC's RFP as an example of relevant work product. It shows how ChiResearch would validate a forecast before a dashboard publishes one. It is original applied analysis produced for this bid, not work delivered under a client contract. ChiResearch has not held a wastewater-surveillance contract. The activity bands shown throughout are KDHE’s published thresholds applied by us to CDC concentration data — categories generated in this analysis, not levels published by any agency, and in particular not CDC’s current Wastewater Viral Activity Level, which uses a different method. No site is named beyond the identifier CDC publishes; the public release carries no facility names or locations, and we have not attempted to re-identify any. The numbers below are computed, not illustrative — the method is stated precisely enough to re-run.

EXHIBIT C·1

The window, not the measurement, decides what gets reported

Kansas indexes reported levels to the full historical peak. WastewaterSCAN uses a trailing one-year window. Our proposal argues that this is a reported-level difference rather than a measurement difference. Finding. Indexed to the all-time peak, 99.1% of site-weeks fall in the lowest band — the scale carries almost no information, because the 2021–22 maximum is far above anything since. Switch to a trailing one-year window and 5.7% of site-weeks move above “low.” Two cautions we state rather than leave to the reader. The window column moves in one direction only, but that is arithmetic, not evidence: a trailing window is a subset of all prior history, so its peak cannot exceed the all-time peak and a switch can only hold a band or raise it. No inference about bias should be drawn from it. And the two rates above rest on different populations and denominators — 10 sewersheds of paired samples against 79 sewersheds of weekly means — so they cannot be ranked against one another as measures of importance. What both support is narrower and sufficient: each of these choices changes the band a dashboard would publish, at sites across the region, with no measurement error involved. Neither can sit as an undocumented default inside a reporting script.

This result replicates. The same method on CDC's archived release — 63 sewersheds, 8,522 site-weeks, data to September 2025 — gave 542 differences, 6.4%. Every empirical finding on this page held across that change — a result that survives a change of federal source, a rebuilt release and a year of additional data is different evidence from one produced once. Four sites show no disagreement, all of them for want of a year of scored history.

EXHIBIT C·2

Four baselines, scored per sewershed

Our proposal commits in Section 5.2 C to seasonal-naïve and smoothed-trend baselines with rolling-origin backtesting before any model is accepted. This is that test, run on all 79 series. Skill is measured against persistence: positive beats a flat line, negative loses to it.

Finding. The best of the four — an exponentially weighted level with a four-week span — beats a flat line at 73 of 79 sewersheds one week out, but only by 8.4% of absolute error at the median. Seasonal-naïve is far worse than persistence (median skill −55%; it wins at 1 of 79 sites), which is to say there is no usable annual seasonality at the sewershed level in this data — a result worth having before a dashboard ships a seasonal model. Trend extrapolation degrades as the horizon grows, from roughly break-even at one week to −31.5% at four. And at 6 sewersheds nothing beats persistence at all. A regional platform should not run one model; it should run the bench and let each sewershed keep whichever baseline wins there.
EXHIBIT C·3

So the dashboard should not publish a number

Median one-week absolute error is 0.40 log10 units — a factor of 2.5. A point forecast of concentration at that accuracy is not a decision input. Direction is a different question, and the answer is better.

Will next period be higher or lower?

Share of weeks the direction call is right. The 50% line is a coin flip; “assume no change” is what a dashboard does by default.

Are the intervals honest?

An 80% interval should contain the truth 80% of the time. Measured from each method's own past errors, expanding window.

Finding. The smoothed level calls direction correctly at 64.1% of weeks one week out against 50.4% for assuming no change, and it is still above chance at four weeks (60.0%) where trend extrapolation has fallen to 51.3% — a coin flip. Meanwhile the nominal 80% interval actually contains the truth 76.4% of the time at one week: the intervals are too narrow, and a dashboard that drew them as published would understate its own uncertainty by about four points. Both results point the same way. Publish a direction and a band with a calibrated interval; do not publish a predicted concentration.

DESIGN RESPONSE

What these four findings require of the platform

01 — TASK AREA 2

Window is a stored parameter

The peak-indexing window is versioned data, not a convention in a script. The platform can then present the Kansas and the SCAN reading side by side and explain the 4.9% gap instead of inheriting one of them silently — and the normalization basis is a second such parameter beside it.

02 — TASK AREA 3

Per-sewershed model registry

No single forecaster wins everywhere, and at six sewersheds none wins at all. Each series carries its own selected baseline, its backtest date and its measured skill — and falls back to persistence when it cannot beat it.

03 — TASK AREA 4

Direction and band, not a number

The dashboard publishes a calibrated direction and level with an interval widened to its measured coverage. A predicted concentration good only to a factor of 2.5 does not go on the screen.

04 — TASK AREA 5

The bench re-runs itself

Skill decays and sewersheds change. This backtest is scheduled, not one-off, and its output is a published table MARC staff can read without us — which is what hosting the platform independently requires.

LIMITS

What this exhibit does not establish

  • These are baselines, not a model. Nothing here tests a mechanistic or machine-learned forecaster against clinical outcomes. The point of the bench is that a model has to clear these bars first, and the bars are higher than they look.
  • No clinical validation. We did not join these series to case counts, emergency-department visits or hospitalizations, so this says nothing about lead time or public-health utility — only about predictability of the measurement itself.
  • The same-sample normalization comparison rests on 10 sewersheds. Only those sites publish both bases with enough paired samples to score. It is a clean comparison on a narrow population, and we do not generalize it past Kansas and Missouri. The forecast scoring uses the flow-population metric only; we did not test whether the choice of basis changes forecastability, which is a question for kickoff.
  • Weekly aggregation is our choice. NWSS sampling is irregular; we aggregated to weekly means to get a regular grid. A different grid would move these numbers, and the grid should be a documented parameter rather than a modelling convenience.
  • The banding rule is Kansas's, applied uniformly. We applied KDHE's published thresholds — half the peak, two-thirds of the peak — to Missouri series too, in order to isolate the window. That is the comparison we wanted, not a claim about how Missouri reports.
  • Attachment 2 measures the same window effect on a different population — raw samples over each series' trailing year with peaks fixed once, rather than weekly means over the whole span with peaks recomputed walk-forward. Different unit, different exposure. We report both rather than reconciling them into one number.
  • Four sites show no window effect, all of them for want of a year of scored history. They are included in the denominator rather than dropped.

Reproducibility. Three scripts produce every figure on this page — pull.py, basis.py and refresh_analysis.py — each taking the cited public release as input and running under Python 3 with numpy and pandas and no other dependency, with no random seed. They are assembled as a package with the retrieved data, the archived-run scripts retained for the replication, recorded dependency versions, expected output and a SHA-256 manifest, available to MARC on request at any point in evaluation.
